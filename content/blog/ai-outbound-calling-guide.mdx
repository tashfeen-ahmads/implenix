<Answer q="Can AI agents make outbound calls, and is it legal?">
Yes. AI voice agents can dial out, hold a real conversation, book appointments and log the result. In the US it is legal when you follow the TCPA: since the FCC's February 2024 ruling, an AI-generated voice counts as an "artificial" voice, so you need the person's prior express consent (written consent for sales calls), must identify your business, and must honor opt-outs. Cold AI calls to consumers without consent are illegal.
</Answer>

Outbound is where I see operators most likely to hurt themselves.
Voice AI makes it technically trivial to dial thousands of
numbers at once, and the temptation to point it at a purchased
list is real. I have declined to build a handful of these
deployments. This is the practical guide to what works and what
does not, from someone who has stayed out of trouble by staying
out of the wrong use cases.

One note before we start: this is a plain-English summary of the
federal rules as I apply them in deployments, with links to the
primary sources. It is not legal advice, and consent language for
your business should be reviewed by your own counsel.

## Can AI agents make outbound calls?

Technically, yes, and they do it well. A modern AI voice agent can:

- Dial a number from a list, a schedule, or a trigger in your CRM
  (a new web lead, a booked appointment, an unpaid invoice).
- Detect whether a human or voicemail picked up.
- Hold a two-way conversation, answer questions, and handle
  "can you call me back at 4?"
- Book, reschedule or confirm in your calendar, then write the
  outcome back to the CRM.
- Transfer to a human when the call needs one.

The hard part is not the technology. It is deciding who you are
allowed to call and what the agent has to say when it does. That
is what the rest of this guide covers. If you want to hear what an
AI agent sounds like on the phone first, [talk to Ron](/try-it),
our demo agent.

## Is it illegal for AI to make phone calls?

No, not by itself. What makes an AI call illegal in the US is
calling without the right consent, or without the required
identification and opt-out. The key facts:

- **AI voices are "artificial" voices.** On February 8, 2024 the FCC
  released a unanimous Declaratory Ruling (FCC 24-17) confirming that
  AI-generated voices, including voice cloning, fall under the
  Telephone Consumer Protection Act's "artificial or prerecorded
  voice" rules. Calls using them need the prior express consent of
  the called party unless there is an emergency purpose or an
  exemption. ([FCC announcement](https://www.fcc.gov/document/fcc-makes-ai-generated-voices-robocalls-illegal))
- **It does not matter that the agent is conversational.** A live,
  back-and-forth AI agent is still an artificial voice. You cannot
  argue your way out of the robocall rules because the bot can
  answer questions.
- **Sales calls need written consent.** For calls that include an
  advertisement or are telemarketing, the FCC's rules require
  *prior express written consent*, both to mobile numbers and to
  residential landlines. ([47 CFR 64.1200](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-47/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-64/subpart-L/section-64.1200))
- **The penalties are per call.** The TCPA lets consumers sue for
  $500 per violating call, up to $1,500 if the violation was willful
  or knowing. State attorneys general can also enforce it, and the
  FCC has used the AI ruling in enforcement: in 2024 it reached a
  $1 million settlement with Lingo Telecom over the deepfake Biden
  robocalls sent before the New Hampshire primary.

So the honest answer to "are AI outbound calls illegal?" is: AI calls
to people who agreed to hear from you, with proper identification
and opt-out, are legal. AI calls to people who did not agree are
illegal, and the exposure multiplies with every number dialed.

A few points that trip people up:

- **"Prior express consent" for informational calls** generally comes
  from the customer knowingly giving you their number for that
  purpose, for example on a booking form. Keep the scope tight: a
  number given to book a cleaning is consent for calls about that
  cleaning, not for a promo campaign.
- **"Prior express written consent"** means a signed agreement
  (an e-signature or web form checkbox counts) that clearly
  authorizes calls using an artificial or prerecorded voice to that
  number, and makes clear consent is not a condition of purchase.
- **An existing business relationship does not replace written
  consent** for artificial-voice sales calls. Being a customer is not
  the same as agreeing to AI marketing calls.
- **Ringless voicemail is a call.** The FCC ruled in November 2022
  that ringless voicemails to wireless phones are calls under the
  TCPA, so the same consent applies.
- **The "one-to-one consent" rule is gone.** In January 2025 the
  Eleventh Circuit vacated the FCC's rule that would have required
  consent to name a single seller (*Insurance Marketing Coalition v.
  FCC*). Your existing obligation to get clear, prior consent did not
  change.

## What an AI outbound call must say

The FCC's rules for artificial-voice messages set out what the
call itself has to include ([47 CFR 64.1200(b)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-47/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-64/subpart-L/section-64.1200)):

1. **Identify the business at the start of the call.** The legal
   name of the business responsible for the call.
2. **Give a callback number** where the person can make a
   do-not-call request during business hours.
3. **For telemarketing calls, offer an automated opt-out** (voice or
   keypress) within two seconds of the identification, which adds the
   number to your do-not-call list and ends the call.
4. **Honor revocation fast.** Under the FCC's revocation rules a person
   can withdraw consent by any reasonable means, and you must honor it
   within 10 business days. In practice I suppress the number the
   moment the agent hears "stop calling me."

### Do you have to say it is an AI?

There is no final federal rule yet that requires the words "I am an
AI." In August 2024 the FCC proposed one: a disclosure at the start of
every AI-generated call, plus AI-specific language in written consent
forms ([FCC 24-84 NPRM](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2024/09/10/2024-19028/implications-of-artificial-intelligence-technologies-on-protecting-consumers-from-unwanted-robocalls)).
At the time of writing I have not seen it adopted as a final rule, and
some states have their own bot-disclosure laws.

My rule is simpler than waiting for regulators: every agent I build
says it is a virtual assistant in the first sentence. There is no
version of "pretend to be human" that is defensible, legally or with
customers. Callers accept the disclosure without friction if it is
delivered warmly.

### Calling hours and Do Not Call

Telephone solicitations are limited to 8 a.m. to 9 p.m. in the
recipient's local time, and sales calls to numbers on the National Do
Not Call Registry need the person's consent or another narrow
exemption. A good AI dialer enforces both
automatically, by time zone, before it places the call.

## The safe zone: calls people asked for

Every outbound use case that ships in my deployments falls into
this category. The recipient gave you their number for this purpose
and the call is about something they started.

### Appointment reminders

Day-before and day-of reminders for scheduled appointments.
Confirmation rates typically improve 15 to 25 percent because the
agent runs the sequence every time without missing. Consent comes
from the booking itself. This is where [dental practices](/ai-receptionist-for-dentists)
and [med spas](/ai-receptionist-for-med-spas) usually start.

### Quote follow-up

After a service provider gives an estimate, a four-touch sequence
(call, SMS, call, SMS) over 10 days. Recovers 15 to 22 percent
of unclosed estimates. The person asked for the quote, so a call
about that quote is informational. If the script starts pitching
other services, it becomes telemarketing and needs written consent.

### Post-service check-in

48 hours after a completed service call. "How did it go, any
issues?" Informational, existing customer. Common for
[HVAC companies](/ai-receptionist-for-hvac-companies) and plumbers.

### Overdue invoice or missed appointment recovery

Existing customer, a specific transaction, straightforward outreach.
Debt collection by a third party adds FDCPA rules on top, so scope
that separately.

### Warranty and product recall notification

Existing purchaser. Frequently legally required to be attempted
in a specific timeframe.

### Membership and subscription renewal

This one sits on the line. "Your membership renews on the 15th" is
informational. "Renew now and get 10 percent off" is marketing. If
the call sells, get written consent at signup that covers AI calls.

## The gray zone: web lead follow-up

If a prospect submits a form on your website and asks to be
contacted, a fast AI callback is the single highest-value outbound
program I build. It is also where consent language matters most.

- **Write the form for it.** The form should say, near the submit
  button, that by submitting the person agrees to calls and texts
  from your business, including calls using an AI or artificial voice,
  at the number provided, and that consent is not a condition of
  purchase. Store the timestamp, IP and form version.
- **How long is that consent good for?** There is no bright-line
  rule. Same-day follow-up is unambiguous. Six months later on a
  lead that never converted is questionable. I use a 90-day window
  as an internal policy.
- **What if the form was for something else?** If it said "get a
  free ebook," you do not have consent for AI sales calls.
- **Texting follows the same rules,** and STOP must be honored
  immediately.

## The unsafe zone: cold AI calls to consumers

Do not do this. I have watched operators try it and get demand
letters within 90 days.

- Purchased consumer lists, never safe.
- Data-broker "opted in" lists, where the consent record usually
  does not survive a challenge.
- "Warm" lists from a third party, where inherited consent almost
  never covers you.
- Any list where you cannot produce the consent record for a
  specific number if asked.

If someone files a class action against your outbound program,
one of the first things requested is the consent record for every
number dialed. If you cannot produce it, you are negotiating from
a weak position.

## B2B outbound: more latitude, still not free

The National Do Not Call Registry protects residential subscribers,
so calls to business landlines have more room. But the
artificial-voice consent rule for mobile numbers applies to any
mobile number, including a business owner's cell. An AI agent
calling a contractor's cell phone with a sales pitch needs consent
just like a consumer call.

Business recipients also complain, file with state attorneys
general, and post recordings. The reputational damage from
aggressive B2B AI outbound can outrun the legal risk.

## State rules on top of the TCPA

The TCPA is federal, and many states layer their own telemarketing
laws on top, several with their own private right of action and
their own definitions of automated calls. Florida's Telephone
Solicitation Act is the best known, and Oklahoma, Washington and
others have their own statutes. These change often, so if your
program calls into a state at any volume, get a current review
for that state before launch. I do not skip this on any deployment.

## A compliance checklist for AI outbound

- Consent captured, scoped, timestamped and retrievable per number.
- Written consent, mentioning AI or artificial voice, for any call
  that sells.
- Business identified and AI disclosed in the opening sentence.
- Callback number and an opt-out the agent recognizes in plain
  speech ("stop calling," "take me off your list").
- Opt-outs suppressed across calls and texts immediately.
- Calling windows enforced by the recipient's time zone.
- DNC registry and internal DNC list scrubbed before every campaign.
- Every call recorded or transcribed and logged to the CRM.

sequences before any number is dialed.

## What NOT to build outbound

- Cold sales calls from purchased lists. Legal risk exceeds any
  possible ROI.
- Political outreach without a dedicated political compliance
  review.
- Sales calls to numbers on the DNC registry without consent.
- Debt collection calls without an FDCPA-compliant script and
  disclosure protocol.
- Any call that impersonates a real person or hides that it is
  automated. That is how voice cloning ended up in front of the FCC
  in the first place.

## Frequently asked questions

**Are AI outbound calls illegal?**
Not by themselves. Under the FCC's 2024 ruling an AI-generated
voice is an artificial voice under the TCPA, so AI calls need prior
express consent, and prior express written consent when they sell.
AI calls with that consent, proper identification and an opt-out
are legal. AI calls without it are not.

**Do I need consent for an AI to call my existing customers?**
For informational calls about something they started, like an
appointment or an open quote, the consent they gave when they shared
their number usually covers it. For sales calls you need written
consent, even if they are already a customer.

**Can I use AI to make cold sales calls?**
Not to consumers without prior express written consent. A
purchased "opted-in" list rarely holds up. The safer pattern is
fast AI follow-up on inbound leads who asked to be contacted.

**Does the AI have to say it is an AI?**
There is no final federal rule requiring the exact words yet. The
FCC proposed one in 2024, and some states have bot-disclosure laws.
Every agent I build discloses it anyway, in the first sentence.

**Can the AI leave voicemails?**
Yes, with identification and a callback number, and only where you
have consent. The FCC treats ringless voicemail drops as calls under
the TCPA.

**What happens if I get a TCPA demand letter?**
Do not ignore it. Preserve your records and talk to counsel. The
right move is not to be in that position in the first place.
