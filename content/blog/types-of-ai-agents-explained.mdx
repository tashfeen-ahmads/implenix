<Answer q="What are the main types of AI agents?">
The classic academic taxonomy lists seven types of AI agents (simple reflex, model-based reflex, goal-based, utility-based, learning, multi-agent, and hierarchical) but the practical taxonomy that matters for business deployments has five: reactive agents (respond to inputs, no memory), task agents (execute a specific goal with tools), conversational agents (voice or chat interfaces with reasoning), autonomous agents (long-running, self-directed workflows), and multi-agent systems (multiple agents cooperating). Most business deployments today are task agents or conversational agents.
</Answer>

The types-of-AI-agents question comes up in two contexts. One is
academic, students and researchers using Russell and Norvig's
textbook categories. The other is business, operators trying to
understand what kind of agent they should deploy. This article
covers both but leans toward the second. The five categories
below are the ones I actually see in production, and they map
cleanly onto the operator's decision about what to build or buy.

## The academic taxonomy in one paragraph

The classic Russell and Norvig taxonomy identifies seven types:
simple reflex agents (respond only to current input), model-based reflex agents (maintain internal state), goal-based agents
(work toward a defined goal), utility-based agents (choose
actions that maximize a utility function), learning agents
(improve through experience), multi-agent systems (multiple
agents interacting), and hierarchical agents (agents with
layered decision structures). These are useful for teaching
computer science. They are not useful for evaluating vendors.

## The practical taxonomy that actually applies

### 1. Reactive agents

Simple. Take input, produce output, no memory of previous
interactions. Traditional chatbots with FAQ responses fall here.
So do most rule-based automation systems.

**When they fit:** Very narrow, high-volume, purely
transactional workflows. Order status lookup. Password reset.
Simple FAQ.

**When they fail:** Anything requiring context across the
conversation. Anything with variance.

**Real business example:** A shipping status bot on a
retailer's website that takes an order number and returns
tracking info. No conversation, no context, no reasoning.

### 2. Task agents

Execute a specific goal using a defined set of tools. Take a
request, decide what steps are needed, call the appropriate
APIs, verify success. This is the workhorse category for
business deployments.

**When they fit:** Any workflow with clear inputs, a defined
goal, and structured tool access. Appointment booking. Lead
qualification. Document generation. Data lookup and update.

**When they fail:** Tasks that require ongoing reasoning across
long time horizons or genuinely open-ended goals.

**Real business example:** A voice agent that answers your
inbound line, decides the caller wants to book an appointment,
checks calendar availability, books the slot, and updates the
CRM. All within one conversation.

### 3. Conversational agents

Voice or chat interfaces that hold a real dialog with users.
Combine reasoning about intent with natural language generation
for output. Modern voice AI falls here.

**When they fit:** Any customer-facing interaction where the
value of the conversation comes from real turn-taking. Support,
intake, sales qualification, service booking.

**When they fail:** Situations requiring silent background
processing or non-conversational output.

**Real business example:** The AI receptionist that answers
your service line, holds a real conversation with the caller,
handles interruption and clarification, and completes the
caller's request or transfers cleanly.

### 4. Autonomous agents

Long-running agents that operate over hours or days, taking
initiative to accomplish complex goals without human
intervention at each step. Also called "autonomous workflows"
or "agentic workflows."

**When they fit:** Complex research tasks, ongoing monitoring,
multi-step processes that span time. Code generation and
review. Long research reports. Monitoring and alerting.

**When they fail:** Anything requiring real-time responsiveness.
Anything where the goal or scope might drift over time and
needs human oversight.

**Real business example:** An agent that continuously monitors
inbound leads, scores them for quality, drafts personalized
outreach, sends the outreach after human approval, and tracks
results across a week-long campaign.

### 5. Multi-agent systems

Multiple agents cooperating on a task. Each agent has a
specialized role and they hand off to each other. Emerging
category with real production deployments starting to appear.

**When they fit:** Complex tasks that decompose naturally into
specialized subtasks. Enterprise customer service (router agent
hands to specialist agents). Software development (planner
delegates to executor agents). Research tasks that require
multiple perspectives.

**When they fail:** Simple tasks. Coordination overhead is not
worth it below meaningful complexity.

**Real business example:** A customer support system where a
router agent classifies incoming tickets, hands billing
questions to a billing specialist agent (with access to billing
systems), technical questions to a technical specialist agent
(with access to product documentation), and complex cases to
human agents. Each specialist has narrower scope but deeper
tools.

## Where most business deployments actually sit

The overwhelming majority of production business agents are
either task agents (backend workflows) or conversational agents
(customer-facing voice or chat). Multi-agent systems are
growing but are still niche. Autonomous agents are useful for
specific complex workflows but not for most business
operations. Pure reactive agents still exist but are being
replaced by task agents that can hold context.

## The intersection with LLMs

All modern versions of these agent types are typically LLM-powered under the hood. The taxonomy is not "LLM vs non-LLM."
It is about how the agent operates in the world, regardless of
what technology powers the reasoning.

- Reactive agent + LLM = chatbot with slightly better response
  quality but same interaction model.
- Task agent + LLM = the standard business agent architecture.
- Conversational agent + LLM = modern voice AI.
- Autonomous agent + LLM = the "agentic workflow" category
  currently trending in enterprise AI.
- Multi-agent system + LLM = specialized agent architectures
  with router-and-executor patterns.

## What matters when choosing an agent type for your business

Two questions determine which agent type fits your use case:

**Question 1: How much of the interaction happens in real time
with a human on the other end?**
- All of it, with turn-taking: conversational agent.
- Some of it, with the agent working in the background between
  human touches: task agent.
- None of it, agent operates independently over time:
  autonomous agent.

**Question 2: How much does the task decompose into specialized
subtasks?**
- Single-scope task: single agent (any of the above types).
- Multiple specialized subtasks that need to coordinate:
  multi-agent system.

Answering those two questions correctly narrows the
architecture decision. Everything else is implementation
detail.

## The four characteristics of a well-scoped agent

Regardless of type, a well-scoped production agent has four
properties:

1. **A clearly defined goal.** Not "help the customer", "book
   an appointment," "resolve a support ticket," "qualify a
   sales lead."
2. **Bounded tool access.** Only the tools the agent needs, no
   more. Every unnecessary tool is a potential misfire.
3. **A clean escalation path.** When the agent hits the edge of
   its scope, what happens next?
4. **Observability.** Every call, message, or action logged and
   reviewable.

Agents missing any of these four properties are the ones that
drift, fail unpredictably, or produce compliance problems.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is a "hierarchical agent"?**
An agent architecture where higher-level agents coordinate
lower-level agents. Useful in complex enterprise deployments.
Effectively a subtype of multi-agent systems.

**What is an "AI agent framework" like LangChain or LlamaIndex?**
Not an agent type. A framework provides libraries and
abstractions for building agents of any type. Choosing a
framework is separate from choosing an agent type.

**Is ChatGPT an AI agent?**
The consumer version is primarily a conversational agent
without deep tool access. GPT with plugins or function calling
enabled becomes closer to a task agent. The line has blurred.

**What is an "AI copilot"?**
Usually a conversational agent embedded in a specific
application, focused on assisting a human user with that
application's tasks. Microsoft Copilot for Word is a
conversational agent scoped to document authoring.

**Which type is best for my business?**
For customer-facing operations (phone, chat, service),
conversational agents. For back-office automation, task agents.
For complex enterprise workflows, multi-agent systems. Start
with the simplest type that solves your problem.

## Where to go from here

The taxonomy is useful for orienting. It is not useful for
making buying decisions. When you evaluate a specific agent
deployment, focus on the four properties (goal, tools,
escalation, observability) rather than the category label. The
agents that fail in production usually fail because they were
under-scoped or under-observed, not because they were the
wrong category.
