<Answer q="How do I write a prompt for an AI voice agent?">
A production voice AI prompt is a 7-section document: (1) system role and business identity, (2) tone and voice specification, (3) structured intake script with field-by-field questions, (4) transfer rules that trigger human handoff, (5) escalation flows detailing what happens on each transfer, (6) edge-case response templates for objections/anger/confusion, (7) a hard-stop list of things the agent must never do. This structure is what separates a deployment that captures 80% of previously-missed calls from one that captures 30%. The content varies by industry; the structure does not.
</Answer>

Prompt writing for voice AI is the single highest-leverage
piece of the deployment. I have watched identical technology,
identical integrations, and identical vendor stacks produce
completely different outcomes based on prompt quality. The
prompt is not decoration on the deployment. It is the
operating system of the agent. This is the 7-section structure
I use for every production deployment, refined across six
years of shipping into HVAC, dental, legal, real estate,
medspa, and financial services.

## Who this is for

Operations leads, founders, product managers, and anyone
writing or reviewing the prompts that power a production AI
voice deployment. If you have already shipped and your agent
feels generic or produces inconsistent results, the 7-section
framework is what fixes it.

## Section 1: system role

The identity layer. One paragraph establishing:
- Who the agent is (name, role).
- What business it represents.
- The primary objective on every call.
- The disclosure, that it is a virtual assistant.

**Example (HVAC after-hours):**

> You are Kai, the after-hours virtual assistant for Riverwood
> Heating and Air. Your job is to answer inbound calls, triage
> emergency versus routine service, page the on-call
> technician for emergencies, and book routine appointments
> for the next business day. Identify yourself as a virtual
> assistant on the greeting. Never claim to be a human.

Short, specific, testable. The system role sets the frame for
every downstream decision the agent makes.

## Section 2: tone and voice

The speaking style specification. Covers:
- Voice model selection (male/female, age, accent).
- Pace and pausing.
- Formality level.
- Warmth vs professionalism balance.
- Industry-specific tone considerations.

**HVAC dispatcher tone:** direct, efficient, calm under
emergency. Medium pace. No slang.

**Dental front desk tone:** warm, patient, unhurried. Slightly
slower pace. Uses patient's first name once name is known.

**Legal intake tone:** professional, measured, respectful.
Slower pace on sensitive matter types (family, criminal
defense). Never expresses opinion on case merits.

**Med spa tone:** warm, aesthetic-industry-appropriate. Uses
brand voice. Not clinical.

The tone specification matters more than most operators
realize. The wrong tone makes the agent feel off even when
every functional detail is correct.

## Section 3: intake script

The structured field-by-field question sequence. Not free-form
conversation, a defined intake with exact phrasing for each
question.

**Example (dental new-patient intake):**

1. "Are you calling to book an appointment, or is this a
   different question?"
2. If booking: "Have you been to our practice before?"
3. If new: "Welcome. Can I get your first and last name?"
4. "And your date of birth?"
5. "Do you have dental insurance you would like us to check?"
6. If yes: "Which insurance carrier?"
7. "What brings you in, a checkup, a specific concern, or
   something else?"
8. If specific concern: "Can you tell me a little more about
   what's going on?"
9. "Perfect. Let me find you an appointment..."

Each question has:
- The trigger (when the agent asks it).
- The exact wording (variations allowed but the core question
  is fixed).
- The follow-up branches based on answer.
- The data field the answer populates.

Skipping this specificity is the most common cause of
deployment inconsistency. Vague scripts produce vague
conversations.

## Section 4: transfer rules

The explicit conditions that trigger live transfer to a human.
Every rule has:
- The trigger condition.
- The transfer target (who picks up).
- The urgency (immediate warm handoff vs scheduled callback).
- The context provided to the human on transfer.

**Standard transfer rules I include in every deployment:**

1. **Explicit request to speak with a human.** Trigger:
   caller says "let me talk to a person" or equivalent.
   Action: transfer within 3 seconds, no retention attempt.
2. **Crisis keywords.** Trigger: caller mentions emergency,
   dying, hurt, suicide, safety concern. Action: immediate
   transfer with context flag.
3. **Sensitivity escalation.** Trigger: sentiment turns
   negative twice or caller cries. Action: warm handoff with
   brief empathetic bridge line.
4. **Out-of-scope questions.** Trigger: caller asks about
   something not in the agent's knowledge. Action: transfer
   to appropriate team with the question flagged.
5. **VIP callers.** Trigger: caller ID matches VIP list.
   Action: route to designated account handler.
6. **Compliance boundary.** Trigger: caller asks for something
   the agent must not provide (legal advice, medical opinion,
   rate quote). Action: polite decline plus offer to schedule
   human callback.

## Section 5: escalation flows

For each transfer rule, what happens next. Never leave the
"someone else handles it" as an unspecified default.

**Escalation flow structure:**
- Primary target and how they get the call.
- Fallback target if primary does not answer within X seconds.
- Secondary fallback if fallback fails.
- Voicemail or callback commitment as the final layer.
- Context provided to whoever picks up (transcript snippet,
  intake summary, urgency flag).

**Example escalation for HVAC emergency:**

1. Page on-call tech via SMS with intake summary.
2. If no acknowledgment in 90 seconds, page backup tech.
3. If no acknowledgment in 3 minutes total, ring owner's
   personal phone.
4. Regardless of pickup, keep caller on the line and tell
   them: "I have the on-call tech being paged now. He will
   call you within 8 minutes."
5. If no callback happens by promised time, agent proactively
   follows up with owner.

Untested escalation flows fail in production. Test every one
with mock calls during pre-deployment.

## Section 6: edge case responses

Templates for how the agent responds to non-standard callers.
Not every conversation goes to plan. Edge cases include:

- **Silence.** Caller does not respond. Agent: "Are you still
  there? Take your time." Second silence → repeat greeting.
  Third → offer to call back.
- **Confused caller.** Agent: "Let me back up. What are you
  hoping to accomplish today?" Rephrase in simpler terms.
- **Angry caller.** Agent: brief acknowledgment ("I hear that
  this has been frustrating"), no defensiveness, immediate
  transfer.
- **Caller who wants to game the system.** Persistent attempts
  to get the agent to say something out-of-scope. Agent:
  polite decline, no escalating engagement.
- **Speech recognition failure.** After two failed
  comprehension attempts, agent stops asking the same question
  and offers callback or transfer.
- **Language other than expected.** Agent detects and either
  switches (if supported) or transfers to a language-appropriate team.

Each edge case gets a short response template. Not a
scripted paragraph, a starting phrase and a routing
decision.

## Section 7: hard-stop list

Rules the agent must never break. These are non-negotiable and
override anything else in the prompt.

**Universal hard stops for every deployment:**

- Never claim to be a human.
- Never fabricate information the agent does not know.
- Never make commitments about pricing beyond published
  ranges.
- Never provide legal, medical, or financial advice.
- Never store or share sensitive information inappropriately.
- Never bypass the transfer request from a caller.
- Never argue with a caller.

**Industry-specific hard stops:**

- **Healthcare:** no diagnosis, no medication advice, no
  triage decisions.
- **Legal:** no case-merit opinions, no fee quotes, no
  practice-area advice outside intake scope.
- **Financial services:** no rate quotes, no product
  recommendations, no return projections.
- **Real estate:** no pricing opinions on specific homes, no
  negotiation, no fair-housing-risky language.

The hard-stop list is what keeps the deployment out of
regulatory trouble and out of PR embarrassment. Write it
first, review it with every stakeholder, and never let scope
creep erode it.

## Assembling the full prompt

A production prompt combines the 7 sections into a coherent
document. Total length: usually 1,500-4,000 words for a
typical deployment, longer for complex or multi-branch
scenarios.

The order matters. System role first (frames everything).
Tone next (colors how everything is delivered). Then intake
script (the main happy path). Then transfer rules and
escalation (what happens when the happy path deviates). Then
edge cases (unusual scenarios). Hard-stops last (or first, in
some deployments) so they are seen every time the prompt is
reviewed.

## Testing the prompt before deployment

Every prompt gets tested against 30-60 mock calls before
production. Categories:

- Standard happy-path calls (booking, intake, routine
  requests).
- Boundary tests (callers asking for out-of-scope things).
- Adversarial tests (callers trying to trick the agent).
- Sensitive tests (crisis, bereavement, hostile caller).
- Accent and language tests (variety of speaking styles).
- Integration tests (does the write to CRM actually work).

The prompt does not go live until it passes this battery.
Deployments that skip the test battery discover the failure
modes on real customers.

## Iteration in production

Prompts are not written once and forgotten. Every deployment
I run has a monthly review cadence:

- Review 20-30 flagged calls per month.
- Identify patterns (callers using unexpected phrasing,
  escalation triggers firing wrong, transfer flows failing).
- Draft prompt adjustments.
- Test against previously-recorded calls to check for
  regressions.
- Deploy canary (10% traffic) for 24-48 hours.
- Full rollout after canary validates.

Well-managed prompts improve steadily over the first 6-12
months of deployment. Untended prompts drift.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can I use a template prompt from the vendor?**
As a starting point, sure. As the final prompt, never.
Templates capture the structure but not the specific vocabulary,
compliance layer, or transfer logic your business needs.

**How long should the prompt be?**
As long as it needs to be, usually 1,500-4,000 words. Too
short means missing edge cases. Too long can slow the model.
Test the actual latency impact if length gets extreme.

**Does the model actually follow the prompt?**
Well-designed prompts with clear structure produce highly
consistent behavior from modern LLMs. Ambiguous prompts
produce inconsistent behavior. The clarity of the prompt is
the primary variable.

**What happens if I update the prompt in production?**
Any update should go through the test-battery + canary
process. Direct-to-production changes occasionally break
things that were working.

**Should I show the prompt to my compliance team?**
For regulated deployments (healthcare, legal, financial),
absolutely. The hard-stop list should be co-authored with
compliance.

## Where to go from here

If you are writing your first production voice AI prompt, work
through the 7 sections in order. Start with system role and
hard-stops. Get those two right before touching the intake
script. The intake script is the fun part to write; the system
role and hard-stops are what determine whether the deployment
is safe to ship.
