<Answer q="How will AI agents change cancer research and oncology?">
AI agents are language-model systems that plan multi-step work and call tools such as literature search, genomic databases, and imaging models. In oncology they are starting to help with literature synthesis, clinical trial matching, imaging support, tumor board preparation, and paperwork. Early studies are promising but small. Oncology is one of the specialties where AI agents show the
clearest early promise, and also one where the hype outruns the
evidence most aggressively. I do not deploy into cancer research or
oncology care directly. That is a specialized field with its own
vendors and regulatory framework. I follow it closely because
operators in adjacent medical fields ask me what is real. This is
the honest read.

## What the researchers actually argued

The phrase in this post's title comes from a 2024 comment in
Nature Cancer,
[How AI agents will change cancer research and oncology](https://www.nature.com/articles/s43018-024-00861-7)
(Lee, Ferber, Rood, Regev, and Kather). Their core argument: the
deep learning tools already used in cancer research need a human to
guide every step of a complex workflow, while agents built on large
language models can plan, execute, and refine multi-step reasoning
themselves by calling the right tools. They also flag that
scientists must think through the ethical and safety consequences.

A later review in Nature Reviews Cancer,
[Artificial intelligence agents in cancer research and oncology](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41568-025-00900-0)
(Truhn and colleagues, including Kather), goes deeper. It describes
agents as systems that use outside tools and software to carry out
sequences of tasks with little human input, cites early uses such as
drug design and proposing treatment strategies for clinical cases,
and notes that many clinicians are still unclear on agents' limits
and on the rules that apply to them.

The difference from older medical AI, in one line: a classic model
does one task (find the nodule). An agent strings tasks together
(read the chart, check the mutation database, search the guidelines,
draft the summary) and decides what to do next. I explain that
general idea in
[what agentic AI is](/blog/what-is-agentic-ai) and
[how RAG, LLMs, and tools fit together](/blog/rag-llm-mcp-ai-agent-architecture).

## What the early evidence looks like

The best-known test so far is from the same research group: an
autonomous agent built on GPT-4 and given oncology tools (imaging
models, mutation tools, guideline and literature search), evaluated
on 20 simulated multimodal patient cases and published in
[Nature Cancer](https://www.nature.com/articles/s43018-025-00991-6).
The tool-using agent reached correct conclusions far more often than
GPT-4 on its own, and usually picked the right tools. But its
guideline citations were not always accurate, and 20 simulated cases
is a small, controlled test. It shows promise, not clinical
readiness.

That is the honest state of the field: strong proof of concept,
little real-world outcome data.

## The five areas where AI agents are adding value

### 1. Literature synthesis

The oncology literature is far larger than any clinician can read.
Agent-style tools can search across it for a specific question and
return a summary with citations, which helps researchers and
clinicians decide what is worth reading in full. It filters. It
does not replace deep reading, and every citation should be checked.

### 2. Clinical trial matching

Matching a patient to eligible trials means checking cancer type,
stage, biomarkers, and prior treatments against long lists of
inclusion and exclusion criteria. Agents can read the chart, extract
those features, and propose candidate trials for the care team to
review, including trials the oncologist may not have known about.
Programs are still measuring how much this changes enrollment; ask
any vendor for their published results.

### 3. Imaging and pathology support

Imaging AI is the most mature area of medical AI. On the
[FDA's list of AI-enabled medical devices](https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/software-medical-device-samd/artificial-intelligence-enabled-medical-devices),
radiology accounts for the large majority of authorizations, and
many of those tools are used in cancer screening and follow-up,
for example flagging suspicious findings on mammograms or measuring
lesions across serial scans. Pathology AI is growing too.

These are decision-support tools. The radiologist or pathologist
reviews the findings and makes the call. What agents add is the
glue: pulling the imaging result, the pathology report, and the
genomics into one summary.

### 4. Tumor board and treatment planning support

Molecular tumor boards review complex cases with genomic profiles.
Agents can prepare the case: summarize the history, surface the
relevant guidelines and trials, and list evidence for the options.
The board's oncologists still decide what to recommend.

### 5. Administrative burden

Oncology carries heavy paperwork: documentation, prior
authorizations, care coordination, survivorship follow-up. This is
where voice and agentic AI are most deployable today:

- Ambient documentation during visits, reviewed and signed by the
  clinician.
- Prior authorization intake.
- Scheduling and coordination across medical, surgical, and
  radiation oncology.
- Scripted patient check-ins that route any reported symptom to the
  care team.

This is the part closest to my own work. I describe the operational
side, and the line it must not cross, in
[how generative AI voice agents will transform medicine](/blog/ai-voice-agents-healthcare-medicine).

## What AI agents do NOT do in oncology

- **Diagnose cancer autonomously.** Imaging AI on the market is
  decision support with a physician in the loop.
- **Make treatment decisions.** Oncologists decide, informed by
  evidence the AI surfaces.
- **Deliver bad news.** That is a human conversation.
- **Handle end-of-life conversations.** Human, entirely.
- **Replace oncology nurses, navigators, or coordinators.** These
  roles are being supported, not replaced.
- **"Cure" cancer.** AI is a tool that can make research and care
  faster. It is not the cure.

If you are a patient: AI-assisted tools mostly reach you through
your care team, not as a product you use directly. Questions about
your diagnosis or treatment belong with your oncologist.

## Drug discovery

Beyond the clinic, AI is used across early drug discovery:

- **Target identification** from genomic and proteomic data.
- **Molecule design**, with generative models proposing candidate
  compounds.
- **Trial design** using historical trial data.
- **Toxicity prediction** before compounds reach human testing.

The reviews above describe agents starting to chain these steps.
Whether that shortens the path to approved cancer drugs is still
being tested, and the timelines are measured in years.

## The regulatory picture

In the US, the FDA regulates much oncology AI as software as a
medical device, particularly anything that analyzes images. Expect
validation studies, labeling of intended use, and post-market
monitoring. Software that only supports a clinician's decision, and
lets them see the basis for a recommendation, may fall under the
FDA's clinical decision support policy instead. I summarize those
criteria in
[agentic reasoning and the AI doctor](/blog/agentic-reasoning-ai-doctor).

## The equity question

AI in oncology can narrow or widen disparities in care.

- **Narrow them** by bringing expert-level decision support to
  community cancer centers.
- **Widen them** if the tools only reach well-funded academic
  centers.
- **Widen them** if training data under-represents some patient
  groups, leading to worse performance for those patients.

Ask vendors how their tools perform across patient populations and
what data they were trained on.

## The near-term outlook

Realistic next few years:

- **Broader use** of proven imaging and documentation tools.
- **Better trial matching**, with automatic re-matching as a
  patient's status changes.
- **More agentic tumor board prep**, if validation studies hold up
  in real settings.
- **More administrative automation**, freeing clinical time.
- **Better patient-facing coordination**, with clinicians handling
  every clinical question.

Not on the trajectory:

- Autonomous diagnosis without physician review.
- An "AI oncologist" replacing human oncologists.
- Overnight cures.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is an AI agent in oncology?**
A language-model system that plans multi-step tasks and calls tools,
such as literature search, mutation databases, or imaging models,
to prepare information for clinicians or researchers. A person
reviews and decides.

**Can AI predict who will get cancer?**
Risk models exist for some cancers and can inform screening
decisions made by clinicians. They are not a diagnosis. Talk to your
doctor about your own risk.

**Can AI read my scan and tell me if I have cancer?**
No. AI-assisted imaging helps radiologists read scans. A radiologist
gives the interpretation to you or your doctor.

**Are oncologists being replaced by AI?**
No. AI is being used to take load off oncologists and help them
handle complexity, not to replace them.

**How is patient data protected?**
Clinical AI in the US operates under HIPAA, and research use has its
own rules and ethics review. Ask any vendor for their data
governance policy.

## Where to go from here

For cancer centers and practices evaluating AI, the practical first
step is not building a custom agent. It is evaluating validated,
appropriately regulated tools for your specialty, and starting with
the lowest-risk work: documentation and administration.
